Trying to evaluate the finances of buying a home vs. renting? Explore your own scenarios and see what renting and investing does to your net worth compared with buying a home. The starting values are placeholders, so replace them with your own.

    What this leaves out
    • Taxes of every kind, such as taxes on investment profits, the mortgage interest deduction, and tax on rental income.
    • Costs of buying and selling a home, such as agent fees and closing costs. Leaving them out makes buying look better than it is.
    • Inflation. Dollar amounts aren’t adjusted for rising prices, so $1M in 30 years buys less than $1M today. Your budget also stays the same every year.
    • Ups and downs. Investments and home prices are modeled as growing at a steady average rate, which real markets never do.
    • Borrowing details. A gap above your budget is treated as a loan at one fixed rate with no credit limits.
    • Rental income is a steady amount with no empty months, repairs, or landlord rules.
    • Things money can’t measure: stability, flexibility, control over your space, and the work of owning.

    Understand the numbers

    Short explanations of the ideas behind the results. The shaded lines use the numbers you have entered, so they change as you do.

    Opportunity cost: why the down payment matters

    Every dollar can only be in one place. Money used as a down payment can’t also be invested in the market, so buying has a hidden price: whatever that money would have grown into. This tool counts that cost by letting the renter keep the down payment invested.

    The monthly budget and borrowing

    You choose one monthly budget. Whichever path costs less each month invests what’s left. If a path costs more than the budget, the gap is treated as a loan, and that debt grows at the borrowing rate you set. This keeps the comparison fair: both paths face the same real-world limit.

    Why growth rates matter so much

    Growth compounds: each year’s growth earns growth of its own. A small difference in yearly rates turns into a large difference over decades. A home is also bought partly with borrowed money, so its growth applies to the whole price even though you only put down part of it.

    What break-even rent tells you

    It answers the question “how high would rent need to get before buying starts to make financial sense?” Every other number stays fixed. If your rent is below the break-even, renting and investing comes out ahead. If it’s above, buying does. It isn’t a prediction, and it moves whenever you change any input.

    Using this tool well

    Start with your real numbers. Then change one thing at a time, or add a scenario to compare two versions side by side. The most useful question is rarely “which one wins?” but “which assumption would have to be wrong for the answer to flip?” Treat the results as what-ifs, not forecasts.
